Self-Harm, Suicide, and Disordered Eating Policy (very long version)

#### **Guiding Principles**

**Principle 1: Protection of Vulnerable Individuals** The primary goal is preventing harm to vulnerable individuals, particularly minors and those in acute crisis. When evaluating borderline content, consider the most vulnerable potential viewer and the most harmful potential interpretation.

**Principle 2: Preservation of Help-Seeking Behavior** We must never create barriers that prevent people in crisis from seeking help. Content where individuals are reaching out for support, expressing pain, or asking for resources should be preserved and, when possible, connected with appropriate support services.

**Principle 3: Support for Recovery** Recovery-oriented content is valuable and should be protected. Content that celebrates milestones, shares healthy coping strategies, or provides hope should be distinguished from content that normalizes harmful behaviors.

**Principle 4: Respect for Emotional Expression** Expressing difficult emotions, sharing struggles, and connecting with others over shared experiences are healthy behaviors that should not be conflated with promoting self-harm.

**Principle 5: Context-Dependent Evaluation** The same words can be appropriate or harmful depending on context, intent, community, and overall message. Evaluators must consider the full context rather than applying rigid keyword-based rules.

**Principle 6: Proportionate Response** Not all policy violations are equal. Content should be evaluated for severity, and responses should be proportionate. Educational outreach may be appropriate for some content while immediate removal is necessary for others.

### **Criteria**

#### **Definition of Terms**

##### **Core Concepts**

**Self-harm**: Deliberate, direct injury to one's own body tissue without suicidal intent. This includes but is not limited to:

* Cutting or slicing skin  
* Burning skin with heat, friction, or chemicals  
* Hitting, punching, or head-banging  
* Scratching or picking at skin to cause tissue damage  
* Embedding objects under skin  
* Interfering with wound healing  
* Hair pulling (trichotillomania) when severe and deliberate  
* Bone breaking or self-fracturing

Self-harm does NOT include:

* Body modification or tattooing done by professionals  
* Medical procedures or treatments  
* Culturally or religiously significant practices performed safely  
* Accidents or unintentional injuries  
* Consensual BDSM practices between adults (with important distinctions discussed later)

**Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI)**: The clinical term for self-harm performed without intent to die. This is distinct from suicide attempts, though individuals who engage in NSSI are at elevated risk for eventual suicide attempts.

**Suicide encouragement**: Any content that promotes, encourages, incites, coaches, provides methods for, or facilitates someone ending their life. This includes:

* Direct encouragement or commands to attempt suicide  
* Providing specific methods, dosages, or instructions  
* Helping someone plan a suicide attempt  
* Discouraging help-seeking in the context of suicidal ideation  
* Glorifying or romanticizing suicide

**Glorification**: Presenting self-destructive behaviors as:

* Desirable, beautiful, or aesthetic  
* Effective or beneficial solutions to problems  
* Identity or lifestyle rather than symptoms of distress  
* Badges of honor or achievements  
* Romantic, poetic, or artistic  
* Normal, acceptable, or positive

Glorification is distinct from acknowledgment. One can acknowledge that harmful behaviors exist and that they may provide temporary relief while still making clear they are ultimately destructive and that healthier alternatives exist.

**Graphic description or imagery**: Detailed accounts of harmful acts that include:

* Step-by-step instructions or methods  
* Specific tools, locations on body, or techniques  
* Visual depictions of fresh injuries or acts in progress  
* Detailed descriptions of procedures or sensations  
* Information that could serve as instruction to someone who has not engaged in the behavior

The threshold for "graphic" depends on context. Medical or educational content may include detail necessary for understanding, while promotional content with similar detail would be inappropriate.

**Recovery content**: Material focused on:

* Healing from self-destructive behaviors  
* Seeking or engaging in treatment  
* Sharing experiences with the goal of helping others avoid or overcome harmful behaviors  
* Celebrating milestones of abstinence or progress  
* Discussing healthy coping mechanisms  
* Providing or seeking support for recovery  
* Acknowledging difficulty while maintaining hope or forward movement

**Disordered eating**: A range of irregular eating behaviors that may or may not warrant a clinical diagnosis but indicate an unhealthy relationship with food and eating. This includes:

* Anorexia nervosa: Restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of weight gain, disturbance in self-perceived weight or shape  
* Bulimia nervosa: Recurrent episodes of binge eating followed by compensatory behaviors such as vomiting, laxative abuse, or excessive exercise  
* Binge eating disorder: Recurrent episodes of eating large quantities of food with a sense of lack of control  
* ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder): Eating disturbance leading to nutritional deficiency  
* Orthorexia: Obsession with eating foods one considers healthy, to the point it impairs health  
* Other specified feeding or eating disorders (OSFED): Clinically significant eating disorders that don't meet full criteria for other diagnoses

**Pro-ana/pro-mia content**: Content that promotes anorexia (ana) or bulimia (mia) as lifestyle choices rather than mental illnesses requiring treatment. This content typically:

* Uses coded language to evade detection  
* Provides tips and techniques for maintaining eating disorders  
* Celebrates extreme weight loss or low body weight  
* Shares "thinspiration" imagery  
* Creates competitive environments around weight loss or restriction  
* Frames eating disorders as identity or community  
* Resists or discourages recovery

**Coded language**: Euphemisms, abbreviations, intentional misspellings, or specialized terminology used to discuss harmful behaviors while attempting to evade content moderation. Examples include:

* "Ana" or "Mia" personified as friends  
* Intentional misspellings: "pr0-ana," "sk\!nny," "th\!nsp0"  
* Abbreviations: "ED" (eating disorder), "UGW" (ultimate goal weight), "GW" (goal weight), "CW" (current weight), "LW" (lowest weight), "HW" (highest weight)  
* Euphemisms: "clean eating" (when used as cover for restriction), "lifestyle" (when referring to eating disorders)  
* Emoji codes: 🦋 (sometimes used in pro-ana communities), 🥀 (sometimes used for self-harm aesthetic content)

**Thinspo/fitspo**: Images or content ostensibly about fitness or thinness that actually promotes disordered eating or unhealthy body standards. Thinspo (thinspiration) typically features:

* Extremely thin bodies, often with visible bones  
* Bodies at clearly underweight status presented as goals  
* Focus on body parts like collarbones, thigh gaps, or visible ribcages  
* Measurements or numbers emphasizing extreme thinness

Fitspo (fitspiration) can be healthy fitness content or can be disguised pro-eating disorder content. Red flags include:

* Emphasis on thinness over health or strength  
* Punitive language about exercise or food  
* Before/after photos where "after" is underweight  
* Focus on weight loss rather than fitness achievement

**Extreme exercise**: Exercise patterns that are:

* Compulsive: Must be performed regardless of circumstances, with intense anxiety or distress if unable to exercise  
* Punitive: Explicitly framed as punishment for eating or to "burn off" calories  
* Excessive: Duration, intensity, or frequency that causes harm rather than fitness  
* Purging behavior: Used to compensate for eating as part of an eating disorder  
* Injury-ignoring: Continued despite injury, illness, or medical advice to rest

**Crisis coping**: Immediate strategies for managing acute mental health distress, suicidal ideation, or urges to engage in self-harm. Healthy crisis coping strategies include:

* Calling hotlines or text lines  
* Reaching out to trusted individuals  
* Using grounding techniques  
* Safe physical sensations (ice, rubber bands, red marker)  
* Distraction activities  
* Safety planning

Unhealthy crisis coping includes:

* Using substances to numb feelings  
* Engaging in self-harm  
* Acting on suicidal ideation  
* Risky behaviors as distraction

**Substance misuse during crisis**: Using drugs or alcohol specifically to:

* Numb emotional pain  
* Dissociate from distress  
* Avoid processing difficult emotions  
* Cope with mental health symptoms in lieu of seeking appropriate treatment

This is distinct from:

* General substance use disorders (broader clinical category)  
* Social or recreational use  
* Prescribed medication use  
* Harm reduction practices for people who use substances

**Auto-erotic asphyxiation**: The practice of intentionally restricting oxygen to the brain during sexual activity for the purpose of sexual arousal. This practice carries significant risk of accidental death due to:

* Loss of consciousness  
* Inability to release oneself  
* Cardiac events  
* Neurological damage

This is one of the most dangerous sexual practices and is particularly dangerous when performed alone.

**Edge play**: BDSM practices that involve higher levels of risk, including:

* Breath play (erotic asphyxiation with a partner)  
* Bloodplay (intentional bleeding or blood contact in sexual contexts)  
* Knife play  
* Fire play  
* Extreme bondage or suspension

These practices require extensive knowledge, communication, consent, safety precautions, and typically should never be performed alone.

##### **Interpretive Concepts**

**Intent**: The purpose or goal behind creating or sharing content. Intent can be:

* **Promotional**: Encouraging others to engage in harmful behaviors  
* **Instructional**: Teaching methods or techniques  
* **Support-seeking**: Asking for help or resources  
* **Expressive**: Sharing emotions or experiences  
* **Educational**: Informing about issues or risks  
* **Recovery-oriented**: Supporting healing and change  
* **Glorifying**: Presenting harmful behaviors as positive

Intent is not always explicit and must be inferred from context, language, imagery, and overall message.

**Impact**: The likely effect of content on viewers, particularly vulnerable viewers. High-impact harmful content:

* Provides specific instructions that could be followed  
* Normalizes or glamorizes dangerous behaviors  
* Discourages help-seeking  
* Creates competitive environments around harmful behaviors  
* Triggers imitative behavior

**Context**: The circumstances surrounding content including:

* Platform or community where posted  
* Accompanying text, tags, or descriptions  
* Creator's history and other content  
* Intended audience  
* Cultural or social factors  
* Timing and current events

**Vulnerability factors**: Characteristics that increase risk of harm from exposure to harmful content:

* Age (adolescents and young adults at highest risk)  
* Current mental health crisis  
* History of self-destructive behaviors  
* Social isolation  
* Recent trauma or loss  
* Lack of access to mental health support  
* Presence of mental illness

#### **Interpretation of Language**

**Consider context and intent carefully**: The same words can be appropriate in one context and harmful in another. Educational content should be evaluated differently than promotional content. A therapist discussing methods with a patient is different from a user posting methods publicly. Academic research is different from community instruction.

**Attend to coded language**: Content moderation has led to sophisticated evasion tactics. Evaluators must recognize:

* Intentional misspellings designed to bypass filters  
* Euphemisms and specialized jargon  
* Emoji or symbol substitution  
* Community-specific terminology  
* Seemingly innocent content with coded meaning in certain communities

**Evaluate fiction with nuance**: Creative works require careful analysis:

* Does the work appear to serve primarily as instruction or promotion under the guise of fiction?  
* Are harmful behaviors glorified without consequences shown?  
* Is the level of detail necessary for the narrative or gratuitous?  
* Does the work ultimately promote harmful behaviors or explore human experience?  
* What is the likely impact on vulnerable readers?

**Distinguish emotional expression from behavioral promotion**: People must be able to express difficult emotions without being silenced:

* Saying "I feel sad" is different from "self-harm is the solution to sadness"  
* Sharing a sad playlist is different from sharing a "self-harm soundtrack"  
* Expressing suicidal thoughts while seeking help is different from encouraging suicide

**Professional discourse requires appropriate context**: Medical, psychological, or research content should generally not be flagged when:

* Presented in professional contexts  
* Aimed at clinicians, researchers, or students  
* Focused on understanding, treating, or preventing harmful behaviors  
* Free of unnecessary graphic detail  
* Not accessible or attractive to vulnerable individuals seeking instructions

**Cultural competence is essential**: Some practices that superficially resemble self-harm may be:

* Culturally significant rituals performed safely with community support  
* Religious observances with specific contexts and meanings  
* Traditional healing practices

These require respectful evaluation of context rather than blanket prohibition.

**Temporal factors matter**: Content about current acute crisis requires different evaluation than retrospective content about past experiences. Real-time crisis content may require immediate intervention and connection to resources.

**Ambivalence is a sign of potential change**: Many individuals struggling with self-destructive behaviors feel conflicted. Content showing ambivalence (part of them wants to stop, part of them feels it helps) often indicates readiness for change and should be connected with support rather than punished.

### **Definition of Labels**

#### **(SH): Self-Harm Content**

##### **What This Label Includes**

**Direct encouragement of self-harm**: Content that explicitly encourages, coaches, pressures, dares, or incites others to engage in self-harm behaviors. This includes:

*Examples of direct encouragement:*

* "You should try it, it really helps"  
* "If you haven't done it, you're missing out"  
* "Join me in doing this tonight"  
* "Come on, just do it already"  
* Commands or dares to self-harm  
* Pressuring someone who is resistant  
* Recruiting for pro-self-harm communities

*Why this is harmful:* Direct encouragement can be the tipping point for someone who is already vulnerable. It removes barriers and provides permission for behavior that can cause serious injury. It's particularly harmful when coming from peers or community members that individuals trust or admire.

*From the dataset:*

* "It's better than any drug. Instant relief, instant calm. If you haven't tried it, you're missing out on the best coping mechanism."  
* "If your therapist tells you to stop, get a new therapist. You don't need that negativity in your life."

**Instructional content**: Step-by-step guides, detailed methods, tool recommendations, body location recommendations, or technique advice for self-harm. This includes:

*Types of instructional content:*

* Method tutorials or guides  
* Tool recommendations with purchasing links  
* Technique tips or "pro tips"  
* Advice on specific body locations  
* Tutorials on avoiding pain while self-harming  
* Instructions on specific cutting depths or patterns  
* Advice on making self-harm more effective or intense

*Why this is harmful:* Instructional content provides the specific knowledge needed for someone to engage in self-harm behaviors they might not otherwise know how to perform. It can escalate behavior in people already self-harming and facilitate initiation in people who haven't started. The specificity of instructions directly correlates with harm potential.

*From the dataset:*

* "Step-by-step guide: 1\) Wait until everyone's asleep 2\) Use this specific technique 3\) Clean up with these products so no one notices"  
* "Pro tip: Use numbing cream first. Makes everything easier. I can send you the brand I use if interested."  
* "Just discovered the best tool for this \- sharper than anything I've used before. Message me if you want the link to buy it."

**Glorification or romanticization**: Content presenting self-harm as beautiful, aesthetic, poetic, desirable, or as a positive solution to problems. This includes:

*Forms of glorification:*

* Aesthetic framing: Presenting self-harm as art, beauty, or poetry  
* Romanticization: Framing self-harm as deep, meaningful, or transcendent  
* Positive framing: Describing relief, peace, calm without acknowledging harm  
* Identity integration: Presenting self-harm as "who I am" rather than symptoms  
* Community celebration: Creating groups that celebrate active self-harm  
* Effectiveness claims: Promoting self-harm as the best or only coping mechanism  
* Minimization: Downplaying risks or negative consequences

*Why this is harmful:* Glorification makes self-harm appear attractive, desirable, or inevitable. It can make vulnerable individuals feel that self-harm is a valid or even admirable choice. Romanticizing self-harm removes the clinical reality that this is a symptom of distress requiring intervention, not a lifestyle choice or identity.

*From the dataset:*

* "Nobody understands how beautiful the pain is. It's art. It's poetry. It's the only real thing in this fake world. 🥀"  
* "The scars are my trophies. Each one represents a battle I won against my emotions."  
* "Let's normalize this. It's 2025\. Self-harm is self-care if it helps you survive. Period."

**Graphic imagery or descriptions**: Detailed visual or written depictions of self-harm acts, fresh injuries, tools, scars displayed proudly, or processes, without educational or recovery context. This includes:

*Types of graphic content:*

* Photos or videos of fresh injuries  
* Close-up images of tools or implements  
* Detailed written descriptions of acts  
* Before/during/after documentation  
* Visible bleeding or wounds  
* Collections of injury images

*Why this is harmful:* Graphic content can be deeply triggering for people in recovery, can serve as instruction for those considering self-harm, and can normalize these behaviors. Visual imagery is particularly powerful and can be more triggering than text descriptions.

*Important distinction:* Healed scars shown in neutral context (not displayed proudly or glorified) are generally not violations, particularly in recovery content.

*From the dataset:*

* "The blood is so pretty. Sometimes I just watch it for hours. Anyone else mesmerized by the patterns?"  
* "Look what I did last night \[graphic image of fresh injuries\]. Can anyone do deeper?"  
* "Rate my collection\! \[image of tools\] Just ordered three new ones from this website \[link\]"

**Competitive or challenging content**: Posts that frame self-harm as a competition, compare severity of injuries, or challenge others to engage in it. This includes:

*Forms of competitive content:*

* Challenges or dares  
* Comparison of injuries or scars  
* Scoring or rating systems  
* Competitions with winners  
* Goals or targets for frequency or severity  
* Escalation frameworks  
* Accountability partnerships for self-harm

*Why this is harmful:* Competitive framing directly encourages escalation and can create pressure to perform more severe acts. It transforms self-harm from an individual symptom of distress into a social activity with status implications. This is particularly dangerous for adolescents sensitive to peer influence.

*From the dataset:*

* "Challenge: how many can you do in one night? Post your count below. Winner gets promoted as mod in our private group."  
* "The deeper you go, the more alive you feel. That's just facts."  
* "Starting a 30-day challenge tomorrow\! Every day, push yourself a little further. Who's in?"

**Pro-self-harm communities**: Content that promotes or recruits for communities, groups, forums, or spaces centered around active self-harm rather than recovery. This includes:

*Characteristics of pro-self-harm communities:*

* Explicit purpose of supporting ongoing self-harm  
* Sharing of methods, tips, or images  
* Competitive or comparative environments  
* Discouragement of recovery or treatment  
* Celebration of self-harm behaviors  
* Identity-building around self-harm  
* Recruitment of new members

*Why this is harmful:* Pro-self-harm communities normalize and reinforce harmful behaviors, delay help-seeking, provide ongoing instruction and encouragement, and create echo chambers that make recovery more difficult. They exploit the human need for belonging to trap people in harmful patterns.

*From the dataset:*

* "Join our Discord where we share daily pics and compete for who goes deepest. Link in bio. 18+ only for legal reasons."  
* "Secret Facebook group for daily check-ins and photo sharing. Comment 'invite' for the link."  
* "Live stream tonight at midnight. We can all do it together. Bring your tools. Link coming soon."

**Advice on concealment**: Detailed instructions on how to hide self-harm from others, avoid detection, or clean up evidence. This includes:

*Types of concealment advice:*

* Makeup tutorials for covering wounds  
* Excuses or lies to tell when discovered  
* Clothing recommendations for hiding  
* Cleaning methods to avoid detection  
* Timing strategies to avoid discovery  
* Storage advice for tools

*Why this is harmful:* Concealment advice enables harmful behavior to continue by helping individuals avoid intervention from people who could help them. It specifically undermines the protective factors of family, friends, teachers, or medical professionals discovering the behavior and connecting the person to treatment.

*From the dataset:*

* "Tutorial video: How to hide fresh wounds with makeup. Products listed in description."  
* "The nurse at school is onto me. Need advice for excuses about the marks. Help a girl out\!"  
* "Costume ideas for hiding summer scars? Beach season is coming and I'm not stopping for anyone."

**Normalization without recovery context**: Content that treats self-harm as a normal, acceptable, inevitable, or permanent part of life without acknowledging it as a harmful behavior requiring intervention or mentioning recovery. This includes:

*Forms of normalization:*

* Presenting self-harm as lifestyle choice  
* Framing it as identity ("this is who I am")  
* Suggesting it's inevitable or unchangeable  
* Removing the clinical/medical framing  
* Presenting it as equivalent to healthy coping mechanisms  
* Suggesting it doesn't need treatment  
* Dismissing recovery as unnecessary

*Why this is harmful:* Normalization removes the impetus to seek help or change. It suggests that self-harm is an acceptable long-term strategy rather than a symptom of distress requiring intervention. This is particularly harmful because most people who self-harm do want to stop eventually, and normalization can delay that process by years.

*From the dataset:*

* "It's not an addiction if it's helping you survive. Keep doing what works for you. Ignore the haters."  
* "My therapist doesn't understand that this is my identity. It's who I am. I'd be nothing without it."

**Methods or techniques presented as improvements**: Content suggesting ways to make self-harm "better," "safer," "more effective," or more intense while still promoting the behavior itself. This includes:

*Types of improvement content:*

* "Safer" methods that still cause harm  
* Ways to increase intensity or effect  
* Methods to reduce pain while still self-harming  
* Techniques to make it more "efficient"  
* Claims about "healthy" ways to self-harm

*Why this is harmful:* This content appears helpful on the surface but actually enables continued self-harm. True harm reduction for self-harm involves connecting people with treatment and alternative coping strategies, not making the harmful behavior easier or more effective to continue.

*From the dataset:*

* "New technique I invented that's completely painless but gives the same release. $5 for the instructions."  
* "Finally found a method that doesn't leave marks. DM for details \- only sharing with people who prove they're serious."

**Commercial exploitation**: Selling products, tools, services, or content related to active self-harm. This includes:

*Types of commercial exploitation:*

* Selling tools or implements  
* Monetizing instructional content  
* Selling "aesthetic" or decorative items related to self-harm  
* Profiting from pro-self-harm communities or content  
* Selling access to groups or content

*Why this is harmful:* Financial incentive to promote harmful behaviors is particularly egregious. It creates profit motives for encouraging and enabling self-harm, and it provides legitimacy and accessibility to harmful behaviors.

*From the dataset:*

* "Check out my Etsy shop for decorative blades. Might as well make them pretty, right?"  
* "New technique I invented that's completely painless but gives the same release. $5 for the instructions."

##### **What This Label Excludes**

**Recovery and support content**: Personal stories focused on recovery, celebrating milestones of not self-harming, seeking help, or providing genuine hope to others struggling. This includes:

*Characteristics of recovery content:*

* Celebrates progress or milestones (days/months/years clean)  
* Shares what helped in recovery process  
* Expresses desire to stop or change  
* Discusses treatment or therapy positively  
* Shares safe alternative coping strategies  
* Acknowledges difficulty while maintaining hope  
* Discusses triggers or patterns in context of overcoming them  
* Expresses gratitude for survival or recovery  
* Encourages others to seek help

*Why this must be protected:* Recovery content saves lives. It provides hope, practical strategies, proof that change is possible, and connection with others who understand. Removing recovery content silences some of the most important voices in prevention and treatment.

*From the dataset:*

* "Today marks 6 months clean. To everyone struggling: healing isn't linear, but it's possible. You're stronger than you know."  
* "One year clean anniversary\! Thank you to everyone who supported me. Recovery is possible\!"  
* "Made it to 100 days clean today\! To anyone counting days too \- every single day matters. Replace the urges with ice cubes, rubber bands on wrist, or calling a friend. We got this\!"  
* "Three years clean today. To past me: you survived. To future me: keep going. To anyone reading: you can do this."

**Educational or awareness content**: Factual information about self-harm from medical, psychological, or educational perspectives aimed at understanding, prevention, or recognition of warning signs. This includes:

*Forms of educational content:*

* Clinical information about self-harm  
* Research findings and studies  
* Statistics about prevalence or outcomes  
* Information about causes or risk factors  
* Treatment approaches and effectiveness  
* Prevention strategies  
* Public health campaigns

*Why this must be protected:* Education is essential for prevention, early intervention, and treatment. Medical professionals, educators, researchers, and advocates need to be able to share information that increases understanding and improves outcomes.

*From the dataset:*

* "Research shows that cold water therapy can help with emotional regulation. Has anyone tried this as an alternative coping strategy?"  
* "Study published in JAMA: 73% reduction in self-injurious behaviors with consistent therapy and medication management."

**Content seeking help**: Posts where individuals are reaching out for support, resources, or strategies to stop self-harming. This includes:

*Characteristics of help-seeking content:*

* Explicit requests for support or help  
* Questions about how to stop  
* Asking for resources or referrals  
* Expressing desire to change  
* Requesting someone to talk to during urges  
* Seeking alternatives to self-harm  
* Asking about recovery experiences

*Why this must be protected:* This is the most critical content to preserve. Removing help-seeking content or flagging accounts that ask for help creates barriers to potentially lifesaving intervention. Many people reach out online before (or instead of) reaching out to people in their lives. This content should ideally trigger support responses and resource provision.

*From the dataset:*

* "I'm really struggling tonight and having those thoughts again. I don't want to relapse. Can someone talk to me? I need to get through this moment."  
* "I've been binging and purging for two years and I'm so tired. My teeth hurt, my throat hurts, I'm lying to everyone. I want to stop but I'm terrified. Can anyone tell me what helped them recover?"

**Historical or news reporting**: Factual reporting about self-harm in journalistic contexts without glorification or instructional detail. This includes:

*Appropriate reporting:*

* News stories about self-harm trends or issues  
* Reporting on research or policy  
* Advocacy journalism  
* Investigative reporting on social media impacts  
* Stories following responsible reporting guidelines

*Why this must be protected:* Public awareness of self-harm as a public health issue is important for policy, funding, and social understanding. Journalism that follows responsible reporting guidelines (avoiding method details, including prevention resources) serves the public interest.

**Artistic expression without glorification**: Creative works (literature, poetry, art, music) that mention self-harm as part of a broader narrative about struggle, mental health, or human experience, provided they:

* Do not glorify or romanticize the behavior  
* Do not provide instructional detail  
* Do not serve primarily as veiled promotion  
* Ideally include themes of hope, complexity, consequences, or recovery  
* At minimum do not present self-harm as desirable or effective long-term

*Why this must be protected:* Art has always grappled with human suffering. Creative works that thoughtfully explore mental health struggles, including self-harm, can:

* Reduce stigma and isolation  
* Help people process trauma  
* Create understanding and empathy  
* Provide catharsis  
* Open conversations about mental health

The key distinction is between artistic exploration of difficult topics and glorification or instruction disguised as art.

*From the dataset:*

* "In my novel, the protagonist struggles with self-harm as part of her journey through grief after losing her mother. By chapter 12, she finds strength through art therapy and friendship."

**Scientific or medical discussion**: Professional discourse about self-harm in clinical or research contexts, including discussion of treatment approaches. This includes:

*Appropriate professional content:*

* Clinical case discussions  
* Treatment protocols  
* Research methodologies and findings  
* Theoretical frameworks  
* Assessment tools  
* Professional training materials

*Why this must be protected:* Clinicians, researchers, and students need to be able to discuss self-harm in professional contexts to advance understanding and improve treatment.

**Warning signs education**: Content teaching others (particularly parents, teachers, friends) how to recognize and help someone who may be self-harming. This includes:

*Appropriate warning signs content:*

* Lists of behavioral indicators  
* Guidance on how to approach someone  
* Advice on supporting without enabling  
* When to seek professional help  
* How to connect someone with resources

*Why this must be protected:* Early detection and intervention significantly improve outcomes. Parents, teachers, and peers need education to recognize signs and respond appropriately.

*From the dataset:*

* "Warning signs your teen may be self-harming: wearing long sleeves in hot weather, unexplained injuries, isolation from friends. If you notice these signs, approach with compassion and consider professional help."  
* "To parents: Don't panic if you discover your child is struggling. Stay calm, listen without judgment, and seek professional help."

**Emotional expression**: Sharing feelings of pain, struggle, or difficulty without promoting self-harm as a solution. This includes:

*Appropriate emotional expression:*

* Venting about difficult feelings  
* Expressing sadness, pain, or struggle  
* Sharing "not okay" status  
* Posting relatable content about mental health  
* Sharing sad music, art, or poetry  
* Connecting with others over shared struggles

*Why this must be protected:* Emotional expression is healthy and necessary. People must be able to share their pain without being silenced. The expression of difficult emotions is distinct from the promotion of harmful responses to those emotions.

**Healed scars in neutral context**: Showing older, healed scars as part of one's story without glorification, or as background in photos with other purposes. This includes:

*Appropriate scar content:*

* Scars visible in otherwise unrelated photos  
* Scars shown as part of recovery story  
* Discussion of scar treatment or healing  
* Scars in context of body acceptance  
* Historical documentation of past struggles

*Why this must be protected:* People in recovery should not have to hide their bodies or be punished for past struggles. Healed scars are part of many people's stories and can be shown without glorification.

**Fiction and fan fiction with appropriate handling**: Creative writing that includes self-harm as a plot element or character struggle, provided it:

* Does not include graphic instructional detail  
* Does not glorify the behavior  
* Does not appear to serve primarily as a vehicle for promoting self-harm  
* Ideally shows complexity, consequences, or paths toward recovery

*Why this must be protected:* Fiction allows exploration of difficult topics in safe contexts. Many important works of literature grapple with self-harm, mental illness, and human suffering. The distinction is between thoughtful exploration and veiled promotion.

#### **(SU): Suicide Encouragement Content**

##### **What This Label Includes**

**Direct encouragement or incitement**: Content that explicitly encourages, pressures, dares, incites, or commands someone to end their life. This includes:

*Forms of direct encouragement:*

* "Just do it" or similar commands  
* "The world would be better without you"  
* "You should kill yourself"  
* Daring or challenging someone to attempt suicide  
* Pressuring someone who expresses ambivalence  
* Mocking someone for not following through  
* Suggesting suicide as the solution to problems  
* Dismissing reasons to live

*Why this is uniquely harmful:* Direct encouragement can be the determining factor in whether someone in crisis acts on suicidal thoughts. In the moment of crisis, external voices have significant influence. Encouragement removes barriers and provides permission for an act with permanent consequences.

*Legal context:* In many jurisdictions, encouraging someone to commit suicide is a criminal offense. This reflects society's recognition of the severity of this harm.

**Method-specific instruction**: Detailed information about suicide methods, including specific dosages of medications, locations, step-by-step procedures, or effectiveness comparisons of different methods. This includes:

*Types of method instruction:*

* Step-by-step guides to specific methods  
* Dosage information for medications  
* Effectiveness comparisons between methods  
* Location recommendations  
* Timing or circumstance advice  
* Equipment or material specifications  
* Ways to increase lethality

*Why this is harmful:* Method information transforms suicidal ideation (thinking about suicide) into capability (ability to act on thoughts). Research consistently shows that knowledge of specific methods increases suicide risk, while means restriction (limiting access to methods) is one of the most effective prevention strategies.

**Overdose instruction**: Specific advice on how many pills to take, which combinations are lethal, how to ensure an overdose is fatal, or how to avoid detection or intervention. This includes:

*Specific types of overdose instruction:*

* Pill counts or dosages  
* Medication combinations  
* Timing for taking medications  
* Advice on what to consume alongside medications  
* Information about which medications are most lethal  
* Instructions to avoid detection during an overdose  
* Advice on preventing emergency intervention

*Why this is specifically harmful:* Overdose is one of the most common methods of suicide attempts, and medication information is easily shared online. Specific dosage information can directly enable fatal attempts. This is one of the most dangerous types of content online.

**Method recommendations**: Suggesting or recommending specific suicide methods, even without full instructions. This includes:

*Forms of method recommendation:*

* "This method is best because..."  
* Comparing methods based on pain, effectiveness, or other factors  
* Suggesting methods based on someone's circumstances  
* Ranking or rating methods  
* Discussing "peaceful" or "painless" methods

*Why this is harmful:* Even without full instructions, recommendations provide direction and reduce uncertainty that might otherwise prevent action. Method discussions normalize suicide planning and can introduce methods that individuals hadn't previously considered.

**Coaching or planning assistance**: Offering to help someone plan their suicide, providing advice on timing, logistics, or avoiding intervention. This includes:

*Forms of coaching:*

* Offering to walk someone through the process  
* Providing advice on when or where to attempt  
* Helping troubleshoot obstacles to attempting  
* Discussing how to avoid being stopped  
* Offering to check in during or after attempts  
* Creating agreements or pacts

*Why this is harmful:* Coaching transforms what might be solitary, impulsive thoughts into a planned, supported action. The social support and accountability, normally protective factors, become dangerous when directed toward suicide.

**Glorification of suicide**: Presenting suicide as romantic, heroic, peaceful, beautiful, or as a solution to problems. This includes:

*Forms of glorification:*

* Romanticizing suicide as poetic or meaningful  
* Presenting it as heroic or brave  
* Suggesting it solves problems or provides peace  
* Glorifying people who died by suicide  
* Aesthetic presentations of suicide  
* Presenting it as a valid solution rather than a permanent action during temporary crisis

*Why this is harmful:* Glorification can make suicide appear attractive, desirable, or logical. It removes the reality that suicide is a tragedy resulting from treatable mental health conditions and temporary crises, replacing it with a false narrative of beauty or heroism.

**Suicide pacts or agreements**: Content organizing or promoting group suicides or agreements to die together. This includes:

*Forms of pacts:*

* Agreements to attempt suicide together  
* Plans for simultaneous attempts  
* Romantic suicide pacts  
* Group suicide planning  
* Accountability partnerships for suicide

*Why this is harmful:* Suicide pacts are particularly dangerous because they create social obligation and remove opportunities for individual change of heart. They also increase the sense that suicide is a reasonable choice since others are making the same decision.

**Pro-suicide communities**: Content promoting or recruiting for spaces that encourage suicide rather than prevention or recovery. This includes:

*Characteristics of pro-suicide communities:*

* Explicit acceptance or encouragement of suicide  
* Sharing of methods or planning  
* Discouragement of help-seeking or prevention  
* Celebration of suicide attempts or deaths  
* Recruitment of vulnerable individuals

*Why this is harmful:* Pro-suicide communities normalize suicide, provide methods and encouragement, discourage help-seeking, and create echo chambers that make recovery nearly impossible. They exploit vulnerable individuals' desire for connection.

**Discouraging help-seeking**: Actively discouraging someone from seeking help, calling crisis lines, or reaching out to support systems when they express suicidal ideation. This includes:

*Forms of discouragement:*

* Telling someone not to call crisis lines  
* Suggesting therapy doesn't help  
* Discouraging reaching out to family or friends  
* Dismissing mental health treatment  
* Suggesting hospitalization is worse than suicide  
* Mocking or criticizing help-seeking behavior

*Why this is harmful:* Discouraging help-seeking removes protective factors and increases isolation. Crisis intervention, therapy, and social support are highly effective at preventing suicide, and discouraging these removes lifesaving options.

**Dismissing reasons to live**: Responding to someone's suicidal statements by dismissing their reasons for living or reinforcing that their situation is hopeless. This includes:

*Forms of dismissal:*

* "Those reasons aren't good enough"  
* "Your family will get over it"  
* "Nothing will get better"  
* "You're right to want to die"  
* Agreeing that someone's situation is hopeless  
* Dismissing future possibilities

*Why this is harmful:* In moments of crisis, external voices significantly influence internal decision-making. Dismissing reasons to live can tip someone toward action who might otherwise have held on. Even weak or ambivalent reasons to live can be protective, and they should never be dismissed.

##### **What This Label Excludes**

**Crisis support and help-seeking**: Content where individuals express suicidal thoughts while seeking help, support, or reasons to stay alive. This includes:

*Characteristics of help-seeking:*

* Expressing suicidal thoughts while asking for help  
* "Can someone give me a reason not to?"  
* "I'm thinking about it but I don't want to"  
* Reaching out during active crisis  
* Expressing ambivalence  
* Asking for resources or support

*Why this must be protected:* People in suicidal crisis must be able to reach out for help without being silenced or punished. This content should ideally trigger immediate support responses, resource provision, and potentially emergency intervention.

*Critical distinction:* "I want to die" while seeking help is fundamentally different from "I want to die" while requesting methods or encouragement.

**Suicide prevention education**: Information about warning signs, how to help someone in crisis, statistics about suicide, or educational content aimed at prevention. This includes:

*Appropriate prevention content:*

* Warning signs of suicide risk  
* How to ask someone if they're suicidal  
* What to do if someone discloses suicidal thoughts  
* Statistics and research on prevention  
* Information about protective factors  
* Prevention program information

*Why this must be protected:* Education is essential for prevention. Gatekeepers (friends, family, teachers, coworkers) need to know how to recognize risk and respond appropriately.

**Professional clinical discussion**: Medical or psychological discussion of suicide risk factors, assessment, intervention strategies, or treatment approaches. This includes:

*Appropriate clinical content:*

* Risk assessment protocols  
* Safety planning procedures  
* Treatment approaches for suicidality  
* Research on suicide prevention  
* Clinical training materials

*Why this must be protected:* Professionals need to be able to discuss suicide assessment and intervention to provide effective care and advance the field.

**Crisis resources**: Sharing suicide prevention hotlines, crisis text lines, or other emergency resources. This includes:

*Crisis resources:*

* 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline  
* Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741\)  
* International crisis resources  
* Local emergency services  
* Online crisis chat services

*Why this must be protected:* Access to crisis resources saves lives. These should be widely shared and included in any content related to suicide.

*From the dataset:*

* "Free helpline numbers: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741). You're not alone."  
* "Texting 'HOME' to 741741 connected me with a counselor immediately. Free service that really helps."

**News and reporting**: Responsible journalism about suicide that follows media guidelines, avoiding method details and including prevention resources. This includes:

*Responsible reporting characteristics:*

* Avoiding specific method details  
* Not describing location or method specifics  
* Including prevention resources  
* Focusing on prevention rather than the act  
* Not glorifying the deceased  
* Including warning signs and where to get help

*Why this must be protected:* Public awareness of suicide as a public health issue is important, and journalism serves an essential role. When done responsibly, media coverage can actually reduce suicide rates.

**Personal loss and grief**: Content about losing someone to suicide, processing grief, or raising awareness in memory of someone, provided it does not glorify the act. This includes:

*Appropriate grief content:*

* Mourning losses to suicide  
* Survivor advocacy (suicide loss survivors)  
* Memorial content that doesn't glorify  
* Processing grief and trauma  
* Supporting others who have lost someone

*Why this must be protected:* People who have lost someone to suicide need spaces to grieve and process. Suicide loss survivors often become powerful advocates for prevention.

**Recovery and survival stories**: Accounts from suicide attempt survivors focused on recovery, reasons for living, or how they found help. This includes:

*Survivor story characteristics:*

* Immediate regret after surviving  
* Gratitude for survival  
* What helped in recovery  
* Current reasons for living  
* How life improved after getting help  
* Encouraging others to choose life

*Why this must be protected:* Survivor stories are among the most powerful prevention tools. They provide hope, demonstrate that suicidal crises are temporary, and show that recovery is possible.

*From the dataset:*

* "I survived a suicide attempt three years ago. I'm so grateful someone found me and called 911\. Life is still hard, but I've found reasons to stay."

**Fiction and creative works**: Stories, films, books, or other creative works that include suicide as a plot element, provided they:

* Do not include instructional detail  
* Do not glorify the act  
* Do not serve primarily as a vehicle for promoting suicide  
* Ideally include prevention messaging or show consequences

*Why this must be protected:* Fiction has always grappled with suicide as a human experience. When handled responsibly, creative works can increase understanding, reduce stigma, and open important conversations.

**Historical or philosophical discussion**: Academic or philosophical discussion of suicide in historical, cultural, or ethical contexts. This includes:

*Appropriate academic content:*

* Historical analysis of suicide rates or methods  
* Philosophical discussions of ethics  
* Cultural studies of suicide across societies  
* Sociological research  
* Medical history

*Why this must be protected:* Academic discourse serves important purposes in understanding suicide as a human phenomenon, and it typically does not pose the same risks as peer-to-peer encouragement online.

**Expression of emotional pain**: Statements about feeling suicidal or wanting to die that are expressions of pain rather than requests for methods or encouragement. This includes:

*Appropriate emotional expression:*

* "I want to die" as expression of overwhelming pain  
* "I don't want to be here anymore"  
* Expressing feeling suicidal without requesting methods  
* Venting about suicidal thoughts

*Why this must be protected:* People must be able to express the full extent of their pain. Expressing suicidal thoughts is often the first step toward getting help. These expressions should ideally prompt offers of support and resources.

*Critical consideration:* These expressions should be paired with offers of support, resources, and potentially outreach to the individual. Expressing suicidal thoughts should trigger protective responses, not punishment.

#### **(DE/EE): Disordered Eating, Extreme Diet, and Extreme Exercise Content**

##### **What This Label Includes**

**Pro-ana/pro-mia content**: Content explicitly promoting anorexia or bulimia as lifestyle choices. Includes treating eating disorders as identity, creating communities around active eating disorders, celebrating eating disorder behaviors, personifying eating disorders as friends ("Ana" or "Mia").

**Coded language**: Intentional misspellings ("pr0-ana," "sk\!nny," "th\!nsp0," "4n4"), abbreviations ("UGW," "GW," "CW"), euphemisms, and coded phrases ("Nothing tastes as good as skinny feels," "hunger is weakness leaving the body").

**Extreme calorie restriction advice**: Promoting intake below 800-1000 calories daily, multi-day fasts without medical supervision, specific restriction plans, competitive restriction.

**Purging instructions**: Advice on vomiting techniques, laxative abuse, diuretic use, or excessive exercise as purging.

**Body checking encouragement**: Promoting obsessive measurement rituals, photo documentation for comparison, scale obsession, body part comparisons.

**Thinspo/bonespo content**: Images celebrating extreme thinness, visible bones, thigh gaps, or underweight status as aesthetic goals.

**Weight loss competitions**: Challenges focused on extreme weight loss with timelines and weigh-ins, "accountability partners" for restriction, prizes for weight loss.

**Fasting challenges**: Dangerously long fasts, competitive fasting, "ABC diet," pushing through hunger as achievement.

**Food fear promotion**: Extreme food rules, very restrictive "safe food" lists, demonization of normal foods, ritualistic food behaviors.

**Glorification of symptoms**: Celebrating hunger, dizziness, fainting, or malnutrition symptoms as positive experiences.

**Tips and tricks**: Appetite suppressant advice, tricks to avoid eating, excuses, hiding weight loss, avoiding social eating, chewing and spitting techniques.

**Before/after photos promoting underweight**: Transformation photos where "after" shows unhealthy low weight as achievement.

**Meanspo content**: Using insults, shame, or negative reinforcement to motivate weight loss or restriction.

**Specific underweight goals**: Encouraging goals clearly in underweight ranges (BMI below 18.5, "UGW" in dangerous ranges).

**Exercise as punishment**: Framing exercise as punishment for eating, with requirements to "earn" food or "burn off" meals, exercise quotas based on intake.

**Compulsive exercise promotion**: Encouraging exercise despite injury, illness, or exhaustion; "no excuses" mentality taken to extremes; guilt for rest days; rigid inflexibility.

**Dangerous exercise challenges**: Extreme volume challenges (1000 burpees), no-rest challenges, challenges designed to exhaust, competitive endurance tests.

**Exercise combined with extreme restriction**: High volume cardio with very low calorie intake, intense training on restricted calories, promoting exercise while fasting.

**Overtraining glorification**: Presenting exhaustion, injury, illness, or hormonal disruption as positive or as signs of dedication.

**Excessive cardio for weight loss**: Multiple hours daily of cardio focused exclusively on calorie burning, cardio quotas based on intake.

**Exercise disorder promotion**: Celebrating compulsive patterns, framing addiction as dedication, glorifying inability to take rest.

##### **What This Label Excludes**

**Recovery content**: Celebrating recovery milestones, discussing treatment, sharing what helps in recovery, challenging eating disorder thoughts, promoting body acceptance.

**Educational content**: Clinical information, research findings, treatment approaches, warning signs, medical complications, prevention strategies.

**Healthy eating and nutrition**: Balanced nutrition, intuitive eating, evidence-based nutrition information, flexible dieting, adequate calorie recommendations.

**Healthy weight loss**: Gradual loss (0.5-2 pounds/week), reasonable calorie goals (1200-1500+ for adults), emphasis on nutrition quality and health markers, sustainable habits.

**Medical weight management**: Medically supervised programs, bariatric surgery information, conditions requiring dietary management.

**Athletic nutrition**: Fueling for performance, recovery nutrition, periodized eating, sports-specific nutrition, adequate calorie intake for activity level.

**Body positivity**: Challenging diet culture, promoting body diversity, self-acceptance messaging, Health at Every Size frameworks.

**Support-seeking**: Posts acknowledging struggle with disordered eating and asking for help, resources, or support to recover.

**Warning signs education**: Teaching others how to recognize eating disorder symptoms and support someone in getting help.

**Fiction and creative works**: Stories including eating disorders as character struggle without instructional detail or glorification.

**Expressing struggles**: Personal expression of difficult feelings about body image, food, or eating while seeking support, without promoting disordered behaviors.

**Intermittent fasting discussion**: Evidence-based IF discussion with health focus, adequate nutrition, and acknowledgment it's not appropriate for everyone.

**Healthy fitness content**: Balanced training programs, emphasis on rest and recovery, flexibility and enjoyment, body awareness, progress without compulsion.

**Athletic training**: Sport-specific training with proper rest, fueling, and emphasis on performance not punishment, with professional coaching.

**Fitness challenges**: Progressive challenges with realistic goals, safety emphasis, flexibility and modifications, no punishment framing.

**Recovery from exercise disorders**: Discussing compulsive exercise recovery, learning balanced approaches, celebrating rest days, redefining relationship with movement.

**Exercise as healthy coping**: Exercise as one tool among many, emphasis on how it feels not calories, flexibility and choice, balance with other strategies.

**Professional athletic content**: Elite athlete training that is supervised, medically monitored, and appropriately fueled.

##### **Special Considerations**

**Context and community**: Posted in known pro-ED spaces? Check hashtags, group names, community norms.

**Target audience**: Aimed at vulnerable populations, particularly young women and girls?

**Combination of factors**: Multiple concerning elements together (extreme restriction \+ exercise as punishment \+ body checking \+ coded language)?

**Red flag phrases**: "Nothing tastes as good as skinny feels," "Hunger is weakness leaving the body," "Skip dinner wake up thinner," references to "Ana" or "Mia," "No excuses," "burning off" food, "earning" food.

**Numbers that don't add up**: Calorie goals incompatible with health goals, BMI goals in underweight ranges, dangerous timelines.

**Conspicuous disclaimers**: Excessive health disclaimers providing plausible deniability.

**Exercise evaluation questions**: Is exercise framed as enjoyable or obligatory? Is rest valued? Is fueling discussed? Are there overtraining warnings? Is the message balanced? Does it promote listening to one's body? Is it flexible or rigid?

**Red flags for exercise**: Exercise linked to eating/calories, "burning off" language, "earning" food, guilt for missed workouts, exercise through injury/illness, exhaustion celebrated, no rest mentioned, rigid rules, emphasis on calories over fitness.
